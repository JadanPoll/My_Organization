# On building a technology community

Suppose a county has thousands of engineers, scientists, technologists, entrepreneurs, educators, and technically curious youth. How much of the knowledge, experience, wisdom, and talent represented by that population actually diffuses throughout the community?

Much of the adult technical population is dispersed across employers, professional commitments, personal projects and confidentiality obligations. Much of the youth population is dispersed across schools and households. What sorts of possibilities emerge if we create an environment in which these people can encounter one another and remain connected over time?

A makerspace is one particularly interesting physical nucleus of this environment. But the makerspace itself is not the entirety of what we are trying to accomplish.

## A community for the adults as well

Many of us will work on different projects, change employers, and develop new interests throughout our lives. It would be nice to have a stable and fulfilling way to remain part of a technical community outside of work.

We could meet at the makerspace, over coffee, on Zoom, or at recurring gatherings. We could invite one another to talk about our work and industries within appropriate confidentiality boundaries, share our own projects, explore unfamiliar fields, and contribute to the next generation.

The point is not solely that adults volunteer for the benefit of youth. The community should be intrinsically worthwhile for its adults, too.

## What might growing up here feel like?

Imagine being exposed to engineers working in semiconductor manufacturing, robotics, aerospace, electronics, biotechnology, and other fields. What are their industries actually like? What sorts of problems are they solving? What instruments and mechanisms exist? What did they work on when they were younger? What would they do differently?

Sometimes simply knowing OF something is sufficient to change the landscape of questions one can ask or ambitions one can conceive of. We need not turn every encounter into a formal lesson.

If many youth pursue different projects, their collective experiences also become a resource. One person may be trying to build a robot while another explores functional 3D printing, another embedded computing, another experimental science. The different projects can generate conversations, demonstrations, collaborations, and opportunities for people to learn from one another.

The equipment and experience of the community ought to give curiosity somewhere to go. A youth's own ambition may activate different combinations of expertise, tools, and resources, without a single person needing to know everything.

## Youth running the makerspace

Our ambition is to have tech youth substantially staff and operate the makerspace, compensating them for actual staff work where resources and applicable requirements allow. Adults can coordinate broader community and countywide responsibilities, including financing, technology, education, partnerships, outreach, and continuity.

Youth might progressively take up maintenance, opportunity boards, speaker coordination, equipment operation, outreach, or new technical initiatives. The organization should offer meaningful responsibilities, not invent titles merely to simulate progression.

There is no need for a conventional vertical promotion system to define someone's development. A relatively flat, lateral structure can allow someone to take on more or different responsibilities as their ambitions and competence grow. Formal governance, safety and accountability are still necessary.

## Board meetings as opportunities for diffusion

We could simply announce that we have a board meeting on a certain date, that two or three youth are welcome to attend, and that rides or travel compensation are available. Let us know by the night before.

Participation is optional. Rotate opportunities when there is more interest than capacity. Youth can observe, ask questions, and bring ideas, rather than having to win a permanent representative position.

The ordinary work of the organization can become a learning surface: financing, negotiating, planning, relationships, disagreement, and the actual mechanics of getting things done. Youth can also bring their own ambitions and perspectives into those discussions.

Confidential agenda items, safeguarding, appropriate transport arrangements, and the distinction between invited participation and legal board membership need to be handled explicitly when implementing this.

## What happens over decades?

Perhaps some youth become engineers, researchers, entrepreneurs, and eventually parents. Perhaps they remain connected, come back to talk about their work, finance projects, mentor others, and help expand the possibilities available to the next generation.

That is an ambition, not yet an observed outcome. What matters is whether the institution actually becomes a community people wish to return to.

We want to accumulate not only equipment, but also relationships, technical capabilities, institutional experience, and people who care about the community.
